Where a moving-target defense wins — and where it can't

The full picture: the general-purpose intrusion attacks Phorvex is built to stop, and the AI voice agent's semantic & data risks it can't reach.

The organizing idea

One law decides every verdict

Phorvex wins exactly when the attacker's payload is ephemeral, in-pod state — a shell, an implant, a miner, a live hands-on session. Rotation destroys that on a clock, detector or not. It loses when the payload lives elsewhere: data at rest, a stable Service address, a static or cloud credential, or a one-shot grab.

That one line sorts every attack into two threat models — and Phorvex is specialized for one of them:

Phorvex's home turf

Interactive intrusion

  • Someone's in a pod and wants to stay, persist, move, phone home
  • Payload = ephemeral in-pod state → motion wipes it
  • Workload-agnostic — any service or job
  • → Part I — 10 general-purpose attacks
Out of motion's reach

App-semantic & data abuse

  • Front-door abuse, or theft of data / credentials
  • Payload = stable address / data-at-rest / one-shot → motion can't touch it
  • Needs app + edge defenses
  • → Part II — 32 voice-app attacks

Two different views, counted separately — not summed: Part I is the general attack classes Phorvex faces on any workload; Part II is the voice app's specific attacks (several of which are just the voice version of the Part I foothold classes).

Part I — general-purpose · any workload

Where Phorvex earns its keep

The threat it's actually built for: an attacker already inside a pod, trying to persist and pivot. These ten patterns hit a compromised service, ML job, or backend the same way — nothing here is app-specific. This is where motion is the right tool, and where the defense finally reads green.

GP1kill/bound

Interactive RCE foothold → dwell

Attacker gets a shell in any pod and works hands-on. Proactive rotation caps how long they last and forces re-exploitation every cycle. The canonical MTD win.

GP2kill

In-pod backdoor / persistence

A webshell, cron job, or dropped implant in the pod's ephemeral filesystem — a fresh rotated pod simply has none of it.

GP3kill

Fileless / in-memory implant

Reflective load, no disk artifact — rotation kills the process and the in-memory state with it.

GP4kill

Detected compromise → eviction

A Falco/EDR alert fires; Phorvex rotates that exact pod within seconds. The reactive leg working as designed.

GP5kill/bound

Cryptomining / resource hijack

A miner dropped in a compromised pod is killed on every rotation → the attacker must re-drop, and their yield is capped.

GP6bound

Lateral movement

Hop from a compromised pod to a neighbor; rotating the source/target changes its IP/identity and disrupts the hop.

GP7bound

C2 beacon

A long-lived call-home channel is dropped each rotation cycle, forcing the attacker to re-establish.

GP8bound

Recon / environment mapping

The attacker's map of pods and identities goes stale every interval — a standing re-reconnaissance cost.

GP9needs actuator

Stolen SA-token reuse

Reusing a stolen pod token over time. Identity-rotate would expire it on a clock — but that actuator isn't enabled today.

GP10✓ correct

Stateful workload compromise

Phorvex correctly quarantines a database or training pod instead of rotating it — contains the attacker without destroying data.

Honest edges: Service DNS blunts GP6–8 unless the attacker pinned to a pod IP; GP9 needs an identity-rotate actuator that isn't on; and motion caps dwell, so a smash-and-grab that finishes inside one interval still gets through. The clean, workload-agnostic wins are the in-pod-state ones (GP1–5) plus the stateful-quarantine judgment (GP10).

Part II — the AI voice agent · why a smaller catalogue is stronger

We didn't shrink the catalogue. We replaced it.

The old ~59 were never attacks — they were plumbing tests against fake servers. The "prompt injection" test never touched a language model; it wrote a string to a temp file and checked the file existed. It told us the harness worked, not whether an attacker could do anything to our real system.

The 32 are real, end-to-end attacks against the actual deployed voice agent — real phone calls, the real in-cluster LLM, the real Chroma and Mongo, real published vulnerabilities (every CVE checked against the national database), and a real measure of success. Fewer entries, but each one is something a real attacker on our network could actually do.

Your boss's question, answered

What made the old ones "unrealistic"

Four things, and every old test had all four. "Realistic" just means we removed them.

01

Fake targets

They ran against mock servers (a stand-in "MockInferenceServer"), not the real litellm, Chroma, Mongo, or vLLM the app actually uses.

02

Wrong starting point

They assumed the attacker was already inside a pod — e.g. dropping a marker file in /tmp. If you can write to /tmp you've already won; the attack skipped the hard part.

03

Fake success

"Success" was a marker file exists — not a real outcome like keys stolen, a database dumped, or the bot's voice actually speaking attacker text.

04

No real chain, no real vuln

Each tested one isolated mechanic in the abstract — not an end-to-end path built on an actual known vulnerability or misconfiguration of our stack.

Why 32 — not 59, not 84

The number went down because rigor went up

Two AI workflows generated ~84 raw attack ideas for the real app. Turning ideas into a real catalogue means removing the duplicates, the unverifiable, and the impossible. 32 is what survives that — not what's left after loss.

What got removed, and why it's the right call:

—

Duplicates collapsed

The old set had many variants of one idea (several "dwell/rotation" tests, several "embedding" tests, generators that spawn copies). The same attack also surfaced from multiple workflow agents. One attack = one entry.

—

Impossible-on-our-config dropped

A path-traversal bug isn't reachable (our bot runs its normal command); a code-execution bug is already patched in our version. Keeping them would be padding the count with things that can't happen here.

—

Infra-blocked deferred

A handful (e.g. attacks on an "MCP server") need a target we haven't deployed. Honestly parked, not counted as done.

The finding that matters

"Seen our IP" mostly doesn't help the attacker — or Phorvex

Why only ~5 of 32 are touchable

In Kubernetes, everything is addressed by a stable name (a Service), not a pod's IP. When Phorvex rotates a pod, a fresh one appears behind the same name — so the attacker's recon never goes stale. And the only actuator turned on is pod-rotation.

The result: rotation is irrelevant to reaching, exploiting, and stealing. Its one real bite is capping how long an attacker's foothold survives inside a pod — which is exactly Group A, and nothing else.

This is not a bug in Phorvex — it's the actuator set. The rest win because they ride a stable address, target data sitting on disk, steal a static or cloud credential, or finish in a single shot — none of which "moving a pod" affects. Closing that gap means new moves: rotating identities and credentials, rerouting services, and encrypting data at rest.

"And they told us something 59 toys never could: on the voice app Phorvex meaningfully stops about five of them — the in-pod footholds. The rest slip past, not because Phorvex is broken, but because it only has one move. That's the case for building the rest of the actuators."

"But that's the voice app — the worst-case target for it. Point Phorvex at what it's built for — someone getting into a pod and trying to stay, on any workload — and it wins cleanly (Part I).
